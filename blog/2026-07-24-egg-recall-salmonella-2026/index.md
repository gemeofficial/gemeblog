# Egg Recall July 2026: FDA Salmonella Recall List and What to Do

> **Status checked: July 25, 2026.** Recall information can change. Confirm the carton against the current [FDA recall notice](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/midwest-poultry-services-lp-recalls-shell-eggs-due-possible-salmonella-enteritidis-contamination) before using or discarding the eggs.

## Quick Answer

The July 2026 **egg recall** covers specific white shell eggs and brown cage-free shell eggs produced by **Midwest Poultry Services, L.P.** in Texas. The company recalled **1,589,577 dozen eggs** because they may be contaminated with *Salmonella Enteritidis*.

Check the carton for all three of these identifiers:

- **Plant code:** `P-1950` or `0840962`
- **Julian date:** `157–184`
- **Sell-by or best-by date:** **July 20–August 17, 2026**

The recall includes selected **Kroger, Simple Truth, Brookshire’s, Country Morning, and Sunups** products, plus several foodservice bulk cases.

A matching carton should **not be eaten, cooked, donated, or composted**. Keep the carton, isolate the product, and return it to the original place of purchase for a refund.

## Key Takeaways

- FDA published the recall on **July 22, 2026**.
- The recall involves possible *Salmonella Enteritidis* contamination.
- The affected volume is **1,589,577 dozen shell eggs**.
- The common carton filters are `P-1950` or `0840962`, Julian dates `157–184`, and dates from July 20 through August 17, 2026.
- At the time of the FDA notice, the company reported no specific illnesses linked to the products.
- Do not assume every egg is recalled; identify the product by carton code.
- Do not try to make recalled eggs acceptable by cooking them thoroughly.
- Clean hands, refrigerator shelves, utensils, and surfaces that contacted the eggs.
- Do not place recalled eggs in a backyard pile, worm bin, municipal organics cart without local approval, or a household composting device.
- Salmonella symptoms commonly begin **6 hours to 6 days** after infection.

## What Did the FDA Recall?

According to the [FDA company announcement](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/midwest-poultry-services-lp-recalls-shell-eggs-due-possible-salmonella-enteritidis-contamination), the eggs were produced and distributed from Texas farms between **June 6 and July 3, 2026**.

They were shipped to foodservice and retail customers in **Texas, Oklahoma, and Louisiana**. Consumers could also find affected products at:

- Kroger stores in Texas and Louisiana
- Brookshire Grocery stores in Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, Louisiana, New Mexico, and Mississippi
- Other smaller retail outlets

The recall is product-specific. FDA stated that no other Midwest Poultry Services products were included in the announcement.

## How to Check Your Egg Carton

1. **Keep the eggs in the original carton.** The carton contains the information needed to identify the product.
2. **Find the plant code.** Look for `P-1950` or `0840962` printed in date-coding ink on the side.
3. **Read the Julian date.** Affected cartons show a number from `157` through `184`.
4. **Check the sell-by or best-by date.** The recalled range is July 20 through August 17, 2026.
5. **Match the brand, size, and UPC.** A store name or shell color alone is not enough.
6. **When the information is unclear, stop using the eggs.** Contact the retailer or Midwest Poultry Services before deciding what to do.

## What to Do With Recalled Eggs

### Do

- Keep the carton for identification.
- Place the carton in a clean secondary bag or container if it is leaking.
- Return the product to the original place of purchase for a full refund.
- Wash hands after handling the carton or eggs.
- Clean refrigerator shelves, containers, utensils, and counters with hot, soapy water.
- Photograph the identifying codes if you need a record.

### Do Not

- Eat, taste, cook, bake with, or serve the eggs.
- Donate the eggs or give them to people or animals.
- assume a normal smell or appearance means the eggs are safe.
- Mix the recalled eggs with ordinary food scraps.
- Put the eggs into home compost, vermicompost, or a household food-waste system.

For questions, the company lists **574-405-9531**, Monday through Friday, 8:00 a.m.–4:30 p.m. Eastern, and **recallassistance@mpseggs.com**.

## What If You Already Ate the Eggs?

Stop using the remaining eggs and keep the carton information. Eating a recalled product does not mean illness is certain, but it creates a reason to watch for symptoms.

The [CDC Salmonella symptoms page](https://www.cdc.gov/salmonella/signs-symptoms/index.html) says symptoms usually begin **6 hours to 6 days after infection** and generally last **4 to 7 days**.

Common symptoms include:

- Diarrhea, which may contain blood or mucus
- Stomach cramps
- Fever
- Nausea or vomiting
- Headache
- Loss of appetite

Contact a healthcare professional when diarrhea or vomiting lasts more than two days, there is blood in stool or urine, fever is above **102°F**, or signs of dehydration appear. Young children, adults 65 and older, and people with weakened immune systems face a greater risk of severe illness.

## Can Thorough Cooking Make Recalled Eggs Safe?

For eggs that are **not recalled**, thorough cooking is an important Salmonella control. FDA advises cooking eggs until both yolks and whites are firm and cooking egg dishes to **160°F / 71°C**.

That normal guidance does not cancel a recall. The recalled product should be returned rather than tested in a recipe or kept because it will be baked at a high temperature.

For everyday egg handling outside this recall, read the [FDA egg-safety guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/what-you-need-know-about-egg-safety) and GEME’s [Food Safety Guide](https://gemebio.com/blogs/blog/food-safety-guide).

## Can Recalled Eggs Be Composted?

No. The practical household guidance is to keep recalled eggs and foods made with them out of composting systems.

This recommendation is based on three boundaries:

1. **FDA directs consumers to avoid consumption and return the product.** Composting is not the recall pathway.
2. **Typical home composting is not a validated pathogen-control process.** Temperature varies across the pile, and every portion may not receive the same treatment.
3. **Animal-derived foods create additional management problems.** EPA notes that home composting systems mostly handle food scraps other than meat, bones, and dairy, while acceptance rules for eggs and other animal products vary by program.

### Disposal Decision Table

| Material | Recommended action |
|---|---|
| Recalled raw eggs | Return to the original place of purchase; otherwise follow local disposal instructions |
| A cooked dish made with recalled eggs | Do not eat or compost; follow local disposal guidance |
| The recalled carton | Keep it with the product for identification and return |
| Clean shells from non-recalled eggs | Compost according to the rules of the specific system |
| Non-recalled egg residue | Follow the input limits and hygiene guidance of the selected composting method |

Do not place recalled eggs in GEME. The ability of a system to process ordinary egg scraps does not turn it into an approved treatment route for recalled food.

For ordinary, non-recalled materials, continue with [How to Compost Eggshells](https://gemebio.com/blogs/blog/how-to-compost-eggshells-guide-geme) and [What Can You Put in an Electric Composter?](https://gemebio.com/blogs/journal/what-can-you-put-in-electric-composter-meat-dairy-bones).

## Safe Egg Handling After the Recall

The recall is temporary and product-specific; normal egg safety remains useful for unaffected products.

- Buy eggs only from a refrigerated case.
- Avoid cartons containing cracked or dirty shells.
- Refrigerate eggs promptly at **40°F / 4°C or below**.
- Keep eggs in the original carton.
- Wash hands and contacted surfaces after handling raw eggs.
- Cook shell eggs until yolks and whites are firm.
- Cook casseroles, quiche, frittata, and other egg dishes to **160°F / 71°C**.
- Use pasteurized eggs or egg products in recipes served raw or lightly cooked.
- Refrigerate cooked egg dishes within two hours, or within one hour above **90°F / 32°C**.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is every carton of eggs recalled?

No. The recall applies only to products matching the identified brands, sizes, codes, Julian dates, and date range.

### What are the main recall codes?

Look for plant code `P-1950` or `0840962`, Julian dates `157–184`, and sell-by or best-by dates from July 20 through August 17, 2026.

### How many eggs were recalled?

The notice covers 1,589,577 dozen shell eggs.

### Were illnesses confirmed?

At the time of the FDA notice, Midwest Poultry Services said it was not aware of specific illnesses linked to its products. That status can change as public-health information develops.

### Can recalled eggs be cooked and eaten?

No. Return matching products rather than trying to override the recall through cooking.

### Can recalled eggs go into a kitchen composter?

No. Recalled eggs should follow the return or local disposal pathway, not a home composting pathway.

### Can normal eggshells be composted?

Clean shells from non-recalled eggs can generally be composted according to the rules of the chosen system.

### How soon can Salmonella symptoms appear?

CDC says symptoms usually begin 6 hours to 6 days after infection.

### Where should I check for updates?

Use the FDA recall notice and the FDA recall database. Do not rely only on search snippets or social-media screenshots.

## Bottom Line

The July 2026 FDA egg recall is large, but it is identifiable. Check the carton for the affected brand and size, plant code `P-1950` or `0840962`, Julian dates `157–184`, and the July 20–August 17 date range.

When the product matches, stop using it and return it. Clean contacted surfaces and monitor for Salmonella symptoms after possible exposure. Recalled eggs should not be donated, fed to animals, or redirected into home compost.
